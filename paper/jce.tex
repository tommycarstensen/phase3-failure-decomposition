\title{How ClinicalTrials.gov is read changes the apparent causes of phase-3 trial failure}

\author{Tommy Carstensen\texorpdfstring{\corref{cor}}{}}
\ead{springer@tommycarstensen.com}
\cortext[cor]{Corresponding author. ORCID 0000-0002-3672-9931.}
\address{Independent researcher, Copenhagen, Denmark}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background:} Registry studies ask why phase-3 trials fail. We asked how two choices in reading ClinicalTrials.gov change the apparent causes of failure.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} This cross-sectional study used \num{cohort.unique} phase-3 registrations that posted results or were terminated, withdrawn or suspended. Failures were trials stopped early for reasons other than benefit and completed trials that missed their primary endpoint. Language-model agents classified stated reasons for stopping; a stronger model, not a human, adjudicated a sample. We varied whether registrations withdrawn before enrollment count as failures, and imputed misses for completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value. A stratum of \num{bigpharma.companies} large companies, chosen after results for a wider list were seen, was compared with Hwang et al.'s manual review.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} Among \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.n} failures of enrolled trials starting in \era, efficacy accounted for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.eff.pct}\% and operational causes for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.op.pct}\% (difference \num{order.enrolled.diff} points, program-clustered 95\% confidence interval [CI] $\num{order.enrolled.diff.lo}$ to \num{order.enrolled.diff.hi}). Counting withdrawn registrations put operational causes first (\num{decomp.registered.era.all.op.pct}\% against \num{decomp.registered.era.all.eff.pct}\%). Only \num{coverage.enrolled.all.reported.pct}\% of completed trials with posted results carried a usable primary $p$-value; imputing misses for the rest with a comparator arm raised efficacy to \num{coverage.enrolled.all.pooled.eff}\%. Among large companies, efficacy accounted for \num{hwang.enrolled.eff.pct}\% (program-clustered 95\% CI \num{hwang.enrolled.clustered.lo} to \num{hwang.enrolled.clustered.hi}), not distinguishable from the \num{hwang.eff.pct}\% of Hwang et al.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} The apparent leading cause of phase-3 failure depends on how withdrawn registrations and completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value are treated. Registry studies should report both choices and the estimate under each; anyone quoting such shares should check both first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
The manual review of Hwang et~al.~\cite{hwang2016} remains the reference point for why late-stage drugs fail. Of \num{hwang.programs} novel therapeutics that entered pivotal trials, \num{hwang.failed} failed, and the failures were attributed to inadequate efficacy (\num{hwang.eff.pct.int}\%), commercial reasons (\num{hwang.com.pct.int}\%) and safety (\num{hwang.saf.pct.int}\%). Their categories have no place for operational causes, although poor recruitment is the most frequently reported reason for discontinuing randomized trials~\cite{kasenda2014} and most reasons for recruitment failure seem preventable~\cite{briel2016}.

Trial registries are increasingly mined for the same question~\cite{teodoro2025}. Williams et~al.~\cite{williams2015} classified the stated reasons of terminated trials with posted results, and Razuvayevskaya et~al.~\cite{razuv2024} classified the stopping reasons of \num{lit.razuv.trials} trials with a fine-tuned language model. In a scoping review of \num{lit.jovanovic.studies} studies of trial failure, most based on ClinicalTrials.gov, Jovanovic et~al.~\cite{jovanovic2026} found reported failure proportions of \num{lit.jovanovic.min.pct}\% to \num{lit.jovanovic.max.pct.int}\% and showed that methodological choices, chiefly whether ongoing trials count as non-failures and which study types are included, account for part of that spread. These studies count or classify failures under one reading of the registry.

We decomposed the causes of phase-3 failure in ClinicalTrials.gov and varied two choices that any such study makes, whether or not it states them. The first is whether registrations withdrawn before enrolling anyone count as failures, and the second is how completed trials that report no usable primary $p$-value are treated. A registry-based share can be set beside the estimate of Hwang et al., or beside that of another registry study, only when both choices are known. We asked whether these choices change the apparent causes of failure, and compared the result for large companies with that estimate, whose unit is a drug program where ours is a trial.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Data, population and failure}\label{sec:data}
In this cross-sectional study, all phase-3 registrations were retrieved from the ClinicalTrials.gov application programming interface (API, v2) on \num{retrieval.cohort} under two filters, posted results ($n=\num{cohort.results}$) and status terminated, withdrawn or suspended ($n=\num{cohort.stopped}$), which gave \num{cohort.unique} unique registrations (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). Phase 2/Phase~3 registrations are included, with no filter on the type of intervention. A registration with status withdrawn stopped before enrolling its first participant. The primary population, called here trials that enrolled, is the \num{cohort.analysed} registrations that were not withdrawn (recorded enrollment counts were not used); counting the \num{cohort.withdrawn} withdrawn ones as failures is the first choice we varied (Choice 1 in Figure~\ref{fig:flow}). The main window is trials starting in \era, after the US Final Rule on clinical trial results reporting took effect~\cite{fda2017}. Start dates are as registered and can be anticipated dates for withdrawn and recent registrations. A completed trial enters the cohort only when its results are posted, so recent starts contribute few completed misses.

A failure is a trial terminated or suspended for a reason other than demonstrated benefit, or a completed trial that missed its primary endpoint. Causes were pooled into five groups, here called buckets, namely efficacy (completed misses and stops for efficacy or futility), safety, operational (enrollment, regulatory, external evidence, \mbox{COVID-19}, supply and other operational reasons), commercial (business or strategic, funding, a bare sponsor decision, and not-started) and unknown (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:buckets}).

\subsection{Primary-endpoint verdict and reporting coverage}\label{sec:verdict}
For trials with posted results we retrieved the primary-outcome statistical-analysis records on \num{retrieval.analyses}. A trial's verdict was a win if at least one primary analysis was significant at \num{alpha} once the comparison operator of its $p$-value string was read, and a miss if every usable analysis was non-significant; a $p$-value is usable when its string places the value on one side of that threshold. Reading the operator matters, because stripping it scores the common `$<$\num{alpha}' style as a miss and changes the verdict of \num{verdict.flips.n} trials (supplement, \S\ref{supp:verdict}).

Completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value have no verdict and cannot enter the efficacy bucket; their treatment is the second choice we varied (Choice 2). Single-arm studies have no between-group test to miss and received no imputation; for trials with a comparator arm (more than one arm group) we imputed the expected number of misses at the miss rate of the reporting ones, taken within the group of sponsors analyzed. As variants, misses were imputed from a logistic model of sponsor class, start year, number of primary outcomes and therapeutic area, and under the bound in which every one missed. Which completed trials had a verdict was modeled by logistic regression with sponsor-clustered standard errors (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:covmodel}).

\subsection{Classification and validation}\label{sec:classification}
Stopped trials were classified by language-model agents with fixed prompts (Claude Sonnet, Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA, run in \num{classification.month}; the version identifiers were not recorded). In the first pass the registry's free-text reason for stopping, the \code{whyStopped} string, was assigned one of \num{valid.categories} categories, that of the primary or first-stated cause where it gave more than one. Registrations with a blank or unclear string went to a second pass, in which a model read the detailed description, and rules labeled bare sponsor decisions, withdrawals without participants and stops after few participants. A third pass read linked PubMed abstracts (supplement, \S\ref{supp:classification}). The prompts and every label are archived, but the batches were assembled interactively and the strings of each batch labeled together, so the classification cannot be re-run and every analysis starts from the archived labels.

A sample of \num{valid.sample} first-pass labels, \num{valid.perCategory} per category (balanced across categories, not representative of them), was labeled again, blind, by a stronger model of the same family (Claude Opus) that saw only the string. No human adjudicated any label, so agreement measures consistency between two models, not accuracy. We report raw agreement with a Wilson interval~\cite{brown2001}, Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960}, agreement weighted to label prevalence, and agreement on the bucket. The later passes and the rules were not validated. Language-model agents, not a human, afterwards read the second-pass model labels of the main window against the registry text, to see whether it supports them (supplement, \S\ref{supp:taxonomy}).

\subsection{Big-pharma stratum, comparison and statistics}\label{sec:strata}
The big-pharma stratum is \num{bigpharma.companies} large pharmaceutical companies and their subsidiaries and acquisitions, matched by name patterns on the lead sponsor (supplement, \S\ref{supp:stratum}). It was narrowed to these companies after results for a wider list with \num{bigpharma.mid} mid-size companies had been seen, which moved the observed efficacy share from \num{hwang.wide.eff.pct}\% to \num{hwang.enrolled.eff.pct}\%, toward the estimate of Hwang et al. The big-pharma efficacy share was compared with that of Hwang et al.\ by a two-sample test of proportions, and again with a program-clustered standard error for this work (a program is the trials that share a subject drug, which are not independent of one another; supplement, \S\ref{supp:subjects}). Shares carry Wilson 95\% intervals, which treat trials as independent; the big-pharma efficacy share and the difference between the efficacy and operational shares of all failures also carry program-clustered ones. Tests are two-sided and descriptive, none is adjusted for multiplicity, and no protocol was registered. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement~\cite{vonelm2007} (supplement, \S\ref{supp:strobe}).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Counting withdrawn registrations}\label{sec:population}
Among trials that enrolled and started in \era\ there were \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.n} failures. Efficacy accounted for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.eff.pct}\% (95\% confidence interval [CI] \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.eff.lo} to \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.eff.hi}) and operational causes for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.op.pct}\% (\num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.op.lo} to \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.op.hi}). The difference, \num{order.enrolled.diff} percentage points, has a program-clustered 95\% CI of $\num{order.enrolled.diff.lo}$ to \num{order.enrolled.diff.hi}, so the two causes are not distinguishable. Commercial reasons accounted for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.com.pct}\%, safety for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.saf.pct}\% and unknown reasons for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.unk.pct}\% (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). For starts in \eracomplete, which have had time to read out, efficacy and operational causes accounted for \num{decomp.enrolled.complete.all.eff.pct}\% and \num{decomp.enrolled.complete.all.op.pct}\% of \num{decomp.enrolled.complete.all.n} failures (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:decompfull}).

Of the \num{cohort.era.stopped} terminated, suspended and withdrawn registrations of the window, \num{cohort.era.withdrawn.n} (\num{cohort.era.withdrawn.pct}\%) were withdrawn, \num{withdrawn.era.n} of them for a reason other than benefit. Operational causes account for \num{withdrawn.era.op.pct}\% of these and efficacy for \num{withdrawn.era.eff.pct}\%, as when another study of the drug had failed. Counted as failures, they bring the denominator to \num{decomp.registered.era.all.n} and put operational causes first, at \num{decomp.registered.era.all.op.pct}\% against \num{decomp.registered.era.all.eff.pct}\% for efficacy, a lead of \num{order.registered.diff} percentage points (program-clustered 95\% CI \num{order.registered.diff.lo} to \num{order.registered.diff.hi}; Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}B, gray markers). A registration that never enrolled anyone is closer to a planned trial than to a failed one, so the main analysis sets it aside.

\subsection{Completed trials without a usable primary \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value}\label{sec:coverage}
Of \num{coverage.enrolled.all.completed} completed, non-stopped trials with posted results in the window, \num{coverage.enrolled.all.reported.n} (\num{coverage.enrolled.all.reported.pct}\%) had a verdict, more often for big-pharma sponsors and less often for single-arm studies and later start years (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:covmodel} and Figure~\ref{fig:reporting}). Stopped trials enter the decomposition whether or not they report a $p$-value, and completed trials enter it only through a reported miss, so the efficacy bucket is under-filled. Among trials with a comparator arm, \num{coverage.enrolled.all.comparator.miss.pct}\% of those with a verdict missed. Applying that rate to the \num{coverage.enrolled.all.comparator.unreported} completed trials with a comparator arm and no verdict adds about \num{coverage.enrolled.all.pooled.extra} misses and gives efficacy \num{coverage.enrolled.all.pooled.eff}\% and operational causes \num{coverage.enrolled.all.pooled.op}\%. The covariate-adjusted imputation gives an efficacy share of \num{coverage.enrolled.all.adjusted.eff}\%, and the bound in which all of those trials missed \num{coverage.enrolled.all.upper.eff}\% (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}A; supplement, Table~\ref{tab:coverage}).

Among randomized superiority trials with posted results, Dechartres et al.~\cite{dechartres2016} computed tests from the posted data of trials that had posted no test, and these were significant less often than the posted tests, which probably makes the imputed efficacy shares too low. Some trials with a comparator arm have no between-group test, which may make them too high. The net direction of that error is uncertain.

The two choices also interact. With withdrawn registrations counted, the imputation leaves efficacy and operational causes level, at \num{coverage.registered.all.pooled.eff}\% and \num{coverage.registered.all.pooled.op}\% (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}B). A stricter win rule, requiring every primary outcome with a usable $p$-value to be significant, also raises the efficacy share, to \num{winrule.and.all.eff.pct}\% (supplement, \S\ref{supp:verdict}).

\subsection{Big pharma against Hwang et al.}\label{sec:hwang}
In the big-pharma stratum there were \num{hwang.enrolled.n} failures among trials that enrolled. Efficacy accounted for \num{hwang.enrolled.eff.pct}\% (program-clustered 95\% CI \num{hwang.enrolled.clustered.lo} to \num{hwang.enrolled.clustered.hi}, \num{hwang.enrolled.clusters} programs), against \num{hwang.eff.pct}\% (\num{hwang.eff.n} of \num{hwang.failed}) in Hwang et al. The difference was not significant (two-sample $p=\num{hwang.enrolled.p}$; program-clustered $p=\num{hwang.enrolled.clustered.p}$), and its clustered 95\% interval, this work minus theirs, runs from $\num{hwang.enrolled.diff.lo}$ to $\num{hwang.enrolled.diff.hi}$ percentage points, so the data do not exclude a difference of several points in either direction (Figure~\ref{fig:hwang}).

The comparison moves with the same two choices. With withdrawn registrations counted, the share falls to \num{hwang.registered.eff.pct}\%, lower than theirs (clustered $p=\num{hwang.registered.clustered.p}$; clustered 95\% interval for the difference $\num{hwang.registered.diff.lo}$ to $\num{hwang.registered.diff.hi}$ points). With unreported misses imputed at the stratum's own miss rate (\num{coverage.enrolled.big.comparator.miss.pct}\%) it rises to \num{coverage.enrolled.big.pooled.eff}\%, above their estimate. This share has a range but no confidence interval, and the range starts at the observed share, because unreported completed trials can only add efficacy failures. With the mid-size companies added, the share is \num{hwang.wide.eff.pct}\% (clustered $p=\num{hwang.wide.clustered.p}$), and among trials of drugs with no earlier phase-3 win in the cohort, the nearest match to the novel therapeutics of Hwang et al., it is \num{hwang.novel.eff.pct}\% (clustered $p=\num{hwang.novel.clustered.p}$; supplement, Tables~\ref{tab:hwang} and~\ref{tab:novelty}). The closeness of the observed share to their estimate is therefore not evidence of agreement.

\subsection{Safety}\label{sec:safety}
Safety accounted for \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.saf.pct}\% of failures among all sponsors and \num{decomp.enrolled.era.big.saf.pct}\% in big pharma, against \num{hwang.saf.pct}\% in Hwang et al. A safety-driven decision can be registered as a sponsor, business or strategic decision. If every bare sponsor decision concealed a safety stop, safety would account for \num{tax.all.sponsorToSafety.saf}\% of failures; if every business or strategic stop did so as well, for \num{tax.all.decisionsToSafety.saf}\% (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}). These are scenarios, not bounds, and the registry cannot show where the truth lies.

\subsection{The labels behind the shares}\label{sec:labels}
Among the stopped trials of the window that were not stopped for benefit, the \code{whyStopped} string alone left \num{recover.enrolled.era.first.noReason.pct}\% without a reason. After the three passes \num{recover.enrolled.era.final.unclear.pct}\% were unclear and \num{recover.enrolled.era.final.reason.pct}\% had a label that states a reason; the rest are bare sponsor decisions, which count as commercial (supplement, \S\ref{supp:classification}). The first-pass label agreed with the stronger model's label for \num{valid.agree.n} of \num{valid.sample} sampled items (\num{valid.agree.pct}\%, 95\% CI \num{valid.agree.lo} to \num{valid.agree.hi}; $\kappa=\num{valid.kappa}$; \num{valid.weighted.pct}\%, \num{valid.weighted.lo} to \num{valid.weighted.hi}, weighted to label prevalence), and the two labels fell in the same bucket for \num{valid.bucket.agree.pct}\%. Agreement was lowest for external evidence and for business or strategic reasons (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:validation}). Of the \num{audit.model.n} second-pass model labels among the stops of the window, \num{audit.eff.n} are efficacy or futility, and a reading by language-model agents, not by a human, found all \num{audit.eff.n} unsupported by the registry text. It also found unsupported labels among the operational ones, and it did not cover other start years. Because that reading is itself unvalidated and partial, no label was changed. Varying five contestable assignments of categories to buckets put the difference between efficacy and operational causes at \num{tax.all.diff.min} to \num{tax.all.diff.max} percentage points, never reversing their order and always inside the clustered interval of the observed difference (\S\ref{sec:population}). The smallest difference, with those \num{audit.eff.n} labels counted as unknown, is \num{tax.all.modelEfficacyToUnknown.eff}\% against \num{tax.all.modelEfficacyToUnknown.op}\% (supplement, Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
A registry-only decomposition of phase-3 failure is sensitive to two choices that are easy to make without noticing. Counting registrations that never enrolled lowers the efficacy share from \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.eff.pct}\% to \num{decomp.registered.era.all.eff.pct}\%. Completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value, \num{coverage.enrolled.all.unreported.pct}\% of those with posted results, cannot enter the efficacy bucket, and imputing misses for those with a comparator arm raises the share by a similar amount, to \num{coverage.enrolled.all.pooled.eff}\%. The direction of each shift follows from the counting, because a registration that never enrolled rarely carries an efficacy reason and an imputed miss is always an efficacy failure; what the registry supplies is the size of the shifts. Neither choice is visible in the output of a decomposition, and the simplest reading of each (counting every withdrawn registration, ignoring the trials without a verdict) makes efficacy look smaller. The number of withdrawn registrations, the share of completed trials with a usable primary $p$-value and the misses of completed trials, observed or imputed, are read from structured fields; the language-model labels enter only through the causes of the stopped trials. All these shares describe what the registry records, not why the drugs failed. Operational causes, \num{decomp.enrolled.era.all.op.pct}\% of the failures of trials that enrolled, are ones a sponsor can act on in a way it cannot act on efficacy~\cite{briel2016}, although the registry cannot show how many were avoidable. For all sponsors, efficacy and operational causes are not distinguishable as observed, and the leads of efficacy under the imputation or a stricter win rule rest on assumptions and were not tested.

Jovanovic et al.\ showed that the statuses counted as non-failures and the study types included change the reported proportion of failed trials~\cite{jovanovic2026}. Our results extend that finding from the proportion of failures to their causes. Counting registrations withdrawn before enrollment is a choice of the kind they examined, the statuses counted; the treatment of completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value is a further one. Both can be checked from registry fields. The definition they recommend counts terminated and withdrawn studies as failed and completed ones as not. Because our comparison with Hwang et al.\ is with drugs that failed, our definition adds completed trials that missed their primary endpoint and sets aside registrations that never enrolled; with withdrawn registrations counted, it is closest to that of Jovanovic et al.

For large companies the observed efficacy share is not distinguishable from the manual estimate of Hwang et al., but the comparison is by proxy and safety is recorded far less often than in their review (\S\ref{sec:safety}). These results describe phase-3 registrations at ClinicalTrials.gov that posted results or stopped early, and do not extend to earlier phases, to trials registered only elsewhere or to completed trials that never posted results.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{lim:validation} \textbf{The validation is model-based.} The adjudicator is a stronger model of the same family, so shared blind spots cannot be ruled out, and the per-category samples are small. The later passes and the rules were not validated, and the later passes labeled \num{valid.era.laterPass.pct}\% of all stopped trials of the window, stops for benefit included. The \num{audit.eff.n} second-pass labels of efficacy or futility that a reading by language-model agents found unsupported were left as the model gave them (\S\ref{sec:labels}). The same reading, which did not cover other start years, found unsupported labels among the operational ones too, and these were not adjusted for. The model versions were not recorded, and the classification cannot be re-run (\S\ref{sec:classification}).
\item\label{lim:imputation} \textbf{The imputation rests on assumptions.} It treats every unreported trial with a comparator arm as able to miss, although two or more arm groups do not guarantee a between-group test, and its model does not use the size of the trial. Completed trials that never posted results are not in the cohort~\cite{anderson2015,devito2020}.
\item\label{lim:population} \textbf{The population is defined by registry status.} Suspended trials, \num{suspended.all.n} of the failures (\num{suspended.all.pct}\%) and none in big pharma, are counted as failures although some may resume. Without them efficacy would account for \num{suspended.all.without.eff}\% and operational causes for \num{suspended.all.without.op}\%.
\item\label{lim:winrule} \textbf{The verdict is lenient.} Any significant primary analysis scores a win, and the direction of effect is not recorded.
\item\label{lim:hwang} \textbf{The comparison with Hwang et al.\ is by proxy.} Their unit is a drug program, counted once, and ours a trial, so our shares weight a program by its number of failed trials. Their programs entered pivotal trials in \num{lit.hwang.firstYear}--\num{lit.hwang.lastYear}, their sources went beyond the registry, and their cohort included smaller companies.
\item\label{lim:plan} \textbf{The analysis was not pre-specified.} The windows, the stratum and the sensitivity analyses were defined during the work, and the stratum was narrowed after results for a wider list had been seen.
\end{enumerate}
Further limitations are in the supplement, Table~\ref{tab:limitations}.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The apparent leading cause of phase-3 failure in ClinicalTrials.gov depends on two choices that are easy to leave unstated, namely how registrations that never enrolled and completed trials without a usable primary $p$-value are treated. A registry study of trial failure should say how it makes both, report the share of completed trials with a usable primary $p$-value, and show its main estimate under each choice. Anyone who quotes a registry-based share of failures, for instance to benchmark a sponsor's attrition, should ask how both choices were made before comparing it with another. A language-model classification of stop reasons should be validated against human readers, which this study could not do.
